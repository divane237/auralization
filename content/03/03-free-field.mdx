## 1. What Is Free-Field Propagation?

A **free field** is an acoustic environment where the source sends energy through space without reflections, diffusion, or scattering. The air stays uniform, the boundaries are far away or perfectly non-reflective, and nothing interferes with the straight-line path between the emitter and receiver.

Typical assumptions:
- no nearby reflecting surfaces  
- a homogeneous, stationary medium  
- an unobstructed line-of-sight path

Free-field models are the foundation for every room-acoustic correction: once you understand how sound behaves without interference, you can layer in reflections, diffusers, and absorbers with intention.

<Callout type="info" title="Free-Field Reminder">
This idealized case is what you compare every complex scenario against—think of it as the reference signal that keeps the math grounded.
</Callout>

---

## 2. Geometrical Spreading of Sound Energy

Sound energy must spread over more area as you move away from the source. For a **point source** radiating equally in every direction, the wavefronts are spheres, and the energy density drops because the energy distributes over the sphere’s surface.

<MathFormula>
{"A = 4 \\pi r^2"}
</MathFormula>

As the radius grows, the same amount of energy covers a larger surface, so the local intensity decreases.

<Callout type="info" title="Energy Conservation">
Geometrical spreading is simply the conservation of energy encoded in space—no energy disappears, it just spreads thinner.
</Callout>

---

## 3. Inverse-Square Law

In the free field, intensity follows the inverse-square law:

<MathFormula>
{"I(r) \\propto \\frac{1}{r^2}"}
</MathFormula>

Because pressure is proportional to the square root of intensity:

<MathFormula>
{"p(r) \\propto \\frac{1}{r}"}
</MathFormula>

The decibel consequence is:

> **Doubling distance cuts sound pressure level by ≈ 6 dB.**

<Callout type="success" title="Distance Rule">
This simple rule stays valid until reflections or absorption dominate, which is why it is the first thing you try when estimating outdoor loudness.
</Callout>

---

## 4. Level Change with Distance

The precise decibel change between two distances is:

<MathFormula>
{"L_p(r_2) = L_p(r_1) - 20 \\log_{10}\\left( \\frac{r_2}{r_1} \\right)"}
</MathFormula>

Examples:
- 1 m → 2 m: −6 dB  
- 1 m → 4 m: −12 dB  
- 5 m → 20 m: −12 dB

This formula ignores frequency-dependent air absorption, so it works best when the medium is essentially lossless.

---

## 5. Spherical vs Plane-Wave Propagation

At the source, energy radiates spherically. Farther away—when the curvature is small relative to the observer—the wavefront is locally flat and the sound behaves like a plane wave.

<ImageSection src="/images/chapter03/subchapt01-5.1.jpg" caption="A source starts as a spherical wave and eventually appears planar to a distant observer." />

| Property | Near-Source (Spherical) | Far-Field (Plane Wave) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wavefront | Curved | Flat |
| Pressure decay | 1/r | Approximately constant |
| Use case | Point sources | Waveguides, distant listeners |

<Callout type="warning" title="Wavefront Caution">
Plane-wave assumptions break down when you’re within a few wavelengths of the source—stay in the spherical regime whenever the source fills the observer’s solid angle.
</Callout>

---

## 6. Atmospheric Absorption

Real air removes energy from the wave through molecular relaxation, viscosity, and thermal conduction. This tiny energy loss converts to heat and increases with frequency.

<ImageSection src="/images/chapter03/subchapt01-6.1.jpg" caption="Absorption climbs at high frequencies, so distant sounds get muffled." />

Key points:
- absorption is frequency dependent  
- significant only after long distances or at high frequencies  
- humidity, temperature, and pressure tune the curve

<Callout type="info" title="Why It Matters">
Outdoor noise prediction and auralization engines always include atmospheric attenuation to keep high-frequency detail from unrealistically propagating forever.
</Callout>

---

## 7. Frequency Dependence of Air Losses

Air attenuation rises rapidly above ~2–4 kHz and depends on the environmental state.

- Low frequencies travel far with little loss  
- High frequencies fade quickly, so distant sources sound muffled  
- Warmer, drier air usually attenuates less than cold, humid air

This is why distant thunder rolls in low and bright chirps disappear sooner.

---

## 8. Combined Effect: Distance + Air Absorption

A practical prediction includes both geometrical spreading and air attenuation:

<MathFormula>
{"L_p(r) = L_p(r_0) - 20 \\log_{10}\\left( \\frac{r}{r_0} \\right) - \\alpha(f) \\cdot (r - r_0)"}
</MathFormula>

Here <MathFormula inline>{"\\alpha(f)"}</MathFormula> is the frequency-dependent attenuation coefficient, so you can tune the model for the current weather conditions.

---

## 9. Limits of the Free-Field Model

The assumptions fail when:
- reflections arrive from boundaries  
- ground effects or wind gradients bend the path  
- diffraction around obstacles changes the trajectory

<Callout type="warning" title="Model-Limit Alert">
Use the free-field result as a sanity check, but switch to room-acoustic or diffusion models once reflections or low-frequency diffraction dominate.
</Callout>

---

## 10. Why Free-Field Propagation Matters

Understanding the free-field baseline lets you:
- separate <strong>source energy</strong> from <strong>room contributions</strong>  
- estimate SPL decay quickly for outdoor layouts  
- feed reliable inputs into auralizations and noise maps  
- detect when atmospheric losses skew the spectrum

---

## 11. Summary

- Free-field propagation assumes no obstacles or reflections—just straight-line energy travel.  
- Energy spreads over spherical surfaces, producing the inverse-square law and the 6 dB-per-doubling rule.  
- Plane-wave behavior emerges far from the source, while near-field remains spherical.  
- Atmospheric absorption is frequency sensitive, so high frequencies disappear first.  
- The combined distance + absorption formula is the practical model for outdoor SPL.  
- When boundaries, diffraction, or gradients appear, switch to more detailed acoustic models.
