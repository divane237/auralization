## 1. Introduction: Why Do We Use Levels?

Acoustic quantities span an extraordinarily wide range of magnitudes.
Some sounds are so weak that they are barely detectable by the human ear, while others are intense enough to cause discomfort or permanent hearing damage. Describing such extremes using ordinary linear numbers quickly becomes impractical.

To illustrate this, consider sound pressure, one of the most fundamental acoustic quantities.
The quietest sound that a healthy human ear can perceive corresponds to pressure fluctuations on the order of only a few tens of micro–Pascals. At the other extreme, very loud sources such as jet engines can generate pressure variations of several hundred Pascals.

<MathFormula> {"p_{\\min} \\approx 20\\,\\mu\\mathrm{Pa} \\qquad p_{\\max} \\approx 200\\,\\mathrm{Pa}"} </MathFormula>

When these two extremes are compared, the resulting ratio is enormous:

<MathFormula> {"\\frac{p_{\\max}}{p_{\\min}} \\approx 10^7"} </MathFormula>

Working directly with numbers that differ by factors of millions is inconvenient and unintuitive.
Fortunately, the human auditory system does not respond linearly to sound energy. Instead, perceived loudness grows approximately logarithmically with physical intensity. This natural characteristic of hearing provides the key motivation for using logarithmic units in acoustics.

By expressing sound quantities as levels, acousticians compress wide dynamic ranges into manageable numbers that align well with perception. As a result, everyday sounds fall into familiar numerical ranges:

Whisper: ~30 dB

Conversation: ~60 dB

Traffic: ~70–80 dB

Rock concert: ~110 dB

The decibel scale therefore serves both practical and perceptual purposes.

## 2. Physical Foundations: Pressure, Intensity, and Power

Before introducing acoustic levels, it is essential to distinguish between three related but fundamentally different physical quantities: sound pressure, sound intensity, and sound power.

### 2.1 Sound Pressure

Sound propagates through a medium as small fluctuations around the ambient (static) pressure.
These fluctuations are described by the sound pressure deviation:

<MathFormula> {"p'(t) = p(t) - p_0"} </MathFormula>

Microphones respond directly to this quantity. However, because sound pressure varies with time, a single instantaneous value is not sufficient to characterize its strength. Instead, acoustics relies on the root-mean-square (RMS) value, which captures the effective magnitude of the fluctuations over time.

<MathFormula> {"p_{\\mathrm{rms}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{1}{T} \\int_0^T \\left(p'(t)\\right)^2 \\, dt}"} </MathFormula>

The RMS pressure is the fundamental quantity used in defining sound pressure levels.

### 2.2 Sound Intensity

While sound pressure describes local pressure variations, sound intensity quantifies how much acoustic energy flows through space. It is defined as the acoustic power transmitted per unit area.

For plane waves, intensity can be expressed as the product of RMS pressure and RMS particle velocity:

<MathFormula> {"I = p_{\\mathrm{rms}}\\,u_{\\mathrm{rms}}"} </MathFormula>

Using the plane-wave relationship between pressure and particle velocity, <MathFormula inline>{"u = \\frac{p'}{\\rho_0 c}"}</MathFormula>

this simplifies to:

<MathFormula> {"I = \\frac{p_{\\mathrm{rms}}^2}{\\rho_0 c}"} </MathFormula>

Sound intensity therefore represents the energy transport of a sound wave.

### 2.3 Sound Power

Sound power describes the total acoustic energy emitted per unit time by a source, independent of distance or environment. It is obtained by integrating the intensity over a closed surface surrounding the source:

<MathFormula> {"W = \\iint I\\, dA"} </MathFormula>

Unlike sound pressure, sound power is a property of the source itself and does not depend on where the receiver is located.

## 3. Why Logarithmic Scales Are Used

The use of logarithmic scales in acoustics is motivated by three key reasons. First, human hearing is approximately logarithmic. A sound must increase in intensity by roughly a factor of ten to be perceived as twice as loud. Second, acoustic quantities span enormous physical ranges, often exceeding factors of 10^12 in intensity. Third, logarithmic representation simplifies calculations. For example, combining two identical independent sound sources results in an increase of only 3 dB, rather than requiring explicit summation of pressures or intensities.

<MathFormula> {"L_{\\text{total}} = L + 3\\,\\mathrm{dB}"} </MathFormula>

## 4. The Decibel Concept

The decibel (dB) is not an absolute unit but a way of expressing the ratio between a quantity and a reference value. In general, a level is defined as:

<MathFormula> {"L = 10\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{Q}{Q_0}\\right)"} </MathFormula>

Because acoustic power and intensity are proportional to the square of sound pressure, pressure-based levels use a factor of 20 instead of 10:

<MathFormula> {"L_p = 20\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{p_{\\mathrm{rms}}}{p_0}\\right)"} </MathFormula>


## 5. Sound Pressure Level (SPL)

The sound pressure level (SPL) is defined using the internationally standardized reference pressure: <MathFormula inline>{"p_0 = 20\\,\\mu\\mathrm{Pa}"}</MathFormula>.

<MathFormula> {"L_p = 20\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{p_{\\mathrm{rms}}}{20\\,\\mu\\mathrm{Pa}}\\right)"} </MathFormula>

This definition maps a wide range of pressures into a compact and interpretable scale.

## 6. Sound Intensity Level

The sound intensity level is defined analogously:

<MathFormula> {"L_I = 10\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{I}{I_0}\\right)"} </MathFormula>

with the reference intensity: <MathFormula inline> {"I_0 = 10^{-12}\\,\\mathrm{W/m^2}"} </MathFormula>

For plane waves, sound pressure level and intensity level are numerically equal: <MathFormula inline> {"L_I = L_p"} </MathFormula>

## 7. Sound Power Level

Sound power level characterizes sources and is defined as:

<MathFormula> {"L_W = 10\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{W}{W_0}\\right)"} </MathFormula>

with reference power: <MathFormula inline> {"W_0 = 10^{-12}\\,\\mathrm{W}"} </MathFormula>

## 8. Level Arithmetic

Because decibels are logarithmic, levels cannot be added directly.
To combine multiple independent sources, levels must first be converted back to linear quantities.

<MathFormula> {"L_{\\text{total}} = 10\\log_{10}\\left(\\sum_i 10^{L_i/10}\\right)"} </MathFormula>

A particularly important result is that doubling acoustic energy produces a 3 dB increase.

## 10. Frequency Weighting

Human hearing sensitivity varies strongly with frequency. To account for this, standardized frequency weighting curves are used in sound measurements.

C-weighting (dBC) preserves low-frequency energy
Z-weighting (dBZ) applies no frequency weighting

## 11. Octave and Third-Octave Bands

Many acoustic analyses require frequency-resolved information.
Rather than using narrowband spectra, acoustics often relies on octave and third-octave bands.

The center frequency of an octave band is given by:

<MathFormula> {"f_c = \\sqrt{f_{\\text{low}} f_{\\text{high}}}"} </MathFormula> <FrequencyBandExplorer />

import numpy as np

p0 = 20e-6  # reference pressure (Pa)
levels = [60, 65, 70]  # SPL values in dB

# convert each level to pressure amplitude
pressures = [p0 * 10 ** (L / 20) for L in levels]

# sum energies assuming incoherent sources
p_total = np.sqrt(sum(p ** 2 for p in pressures))

# convert back to SPL
L_total = 20 * np.log10(p_total / p0)

print("Input levels:", levels)

## 13. Applications

These concepts form the foundation of nearly all acoustic analysis tasks, including environmental noise assessment, room acoustics, loudspeaker design, and auralization.

## 14. Summary

Sound levels provide a compact, perceptually meaningful way to describe acoustic quantities. By expressing pressure, intensity, and power on logarithmic scales, acousticians can analyze complex sound fields efficiently while maintaining a direct connection to human hearing.
