# 1. Introduction: The Importance of Plane Waves

Plane waves are among the most fundamental concepts in acoustics.  
Although *perfect* plane waves cannot exist in a finite room or physical environment, they form the **building blocks** of real sound fields.

Why?

Because:

<Callout type="info" title="Why plane waves again">
Plane waves give us a structured vocabulary for phase, direction, and energy. For many problems we substitute complicated fields with a spectrum of plane waves, so mastering them unlocks every wave-based simulation or auralization pipeline.
</Callout>

### ✔ The wave equation admits plane waves as exact solutions  
### ✔ Any complex sound field can be decomposed into a sum of plane waves  
### ✔ They capture the essence of wave propagation:  
<div className="plane-wave-attributes-card">
  <ul className="plane-wave-attributes-grid">
    <li>phase</li>
    <li>direction</li>
    <li>amplitude</li>
    <li>energy flow</li>
    <li>wavelength</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### ✔ They naturally lead to the **Helmholtz equation** and **frequency-domain acoustics**  
### ✔ They are the basis for:  
Geometrical acoustics, Boundary conditions, Scattering, HRTFs, Auralization algorithms and Room modes.  

In many acoustic systems—ducts, waveguides, long corridors—the lowest-order mode is **almost exactly a plane wave**.

Before exploring room acoustics and signal processing, you must first master the plane wave.

---

# 2. Physical Picture: What Is a Plane Wave?

A plane wave consists of **parallel planar surfaces** (wavefronts) that carry constant phase.  
At a given time, all points on one plane have identical acoustic pressure.

Represented visually:

<ImageSection src="/images/chapter01/subchp03-2.png" caption="Illustration of plane wavefronts" />

Key features:

Wavefronts are straight parallel lines (in 2D) or parallel planes (in 3D).

The wave propagates perpendicularly to these planes.

Pressure varies only with distance along the direction of propagation.

There is no variation laterally.

You may imagine a synchronized “wall” of pressure moving through space.

<Callout type="warning" title="Ideal yet instructive">
  Real environments only host partial plane waves, so use this picture as an organizing principle—plane waves dominate when you are in the far-field or when you isolate a single propagation direction.
</Callout>

<div className="plane-wave-requirements">
  <p className="plane-wave-requirements-label">Plane waves require</p>
  <ul className="plane-wave-requirements-list">
    <li>A homogeneous medium</li>
    <li>No obstacles</li>
    <li>Sufficient distance from the source (far-field region)</li>
  </ul>
</div>

In reality, spherical waves radiated from sources approximate plane waves when sufficiently far.

# 3. Mathematical Representation of Plane Waves

The wave equation in 1D:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\frac{\partial^2 p'}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 p'}{\partial t^2}`}</MathFormula>

has solutions of the form:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`p'(x,t) = A \cos(\omega t - kx + \varphi).`}</MathFormula>

Where:

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`A`}</MathFormula> = amplitude

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\omega = 2\pi f`}</MathFormula> (angular frequency)

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`k = \frac{\omega}{c}`}</MathFormula> (wave number)

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k}`}</MathFormula> (wavelength)

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\varphi`}</MathFormula> = phase offset

This expresses a wave traveling in the +x direction.

## 3.1 Complex Exponential Form (Elegant and Powerful)

Using Euler’s identity:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`p'(x,t) = \Re \{ \tilde{P} e^{j(\omega t - kx)} \}`}</MathFormula>

where:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\tilde{P} = A e^{j\varphi}`}</MathFormula>

This form is deeply useful for:

<div className="plane-wave-grid">
  <ul className="plane-wave-grid-list">
    <li>frequency-domain acoustics</li>
    <li>wave superposition</li>
    <li>scattering</li>
    <li>linear systems</li>
    <li>room modal analysis</li>
  </ul>
</div>

It allows us to treat complex pressure fields as phasors.

---

# 4. Plane Waves in 3D: The Wave Vector

In 3D, a plane wave generalizes to:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`p'(\mathbf{r}, t) = A \cos(\omega t - \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r} + \varphi)`}</MathFormula>

where:

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\mathbf{r} = (x,y,z)`}</MathFormula>

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\mathbf{k}`}</MathFormula> = wave vector

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`|\mathbf{k}| = k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}`}</MathFormula>

The dot product:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} = k_x x + k_y y + k_z z`}</MathFormula>

encodes how phase accumulates in any direction.

✔ Direction of propagation = direction of <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\mathbf{k}`}</MathFormula>
✔ Speed of propagation = <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`c`}</MathFormula>
✔ Wavelength = <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\frac{2\pi}{k}`}</MathFormula>

This representation is essential in:

<div className="plane-wave-grid">
  <ul className="plane-wave-grid-list">
    <li>room acoustics</li>
    <li>boundary reflections</li>
    <li>modal expansions</li>
    <li>wave-based simulation (FDTD / FEM)</li>
    <li>beamforming</li>
    <li>spatial audio rendering</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## 4.1 Direction Cosines

We can express <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\mathbf{k}`}</MathFormula> components as:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`k_x = k \cos(\theta_x), \quad k_y = k \cos(\theta_y), \quad k_z = k \cos(\theta_z).`}</MathFormula>

These cosines describe how the wave travels in each coordinate direction.

---

# 5. Propagation Speed: Deriving the Speed of Sound

The speed of sound is determined by the medium’s elasticity and inertia.

In adiabatic conditions:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`c = \sqrt{\gamma R T}`}</MathFormula>

In general fluid mechanics:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`c = \sqrt{\frac{K}{\rho_0}}`}</MathFormula>

Where:

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`K`}</MathFormula> = bulk modulus

<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\rho_0`}</MathFormula> = equilibrium density

This explains:

Sound travels faster in stiff media (high <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`K`}</MathFormula>).

Sound travels slower in dense media (high <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\rho_0`}</MathFormula>).

Typical speeds:

Air (20°C): <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`c \approx 343 \text{ m/s}`}</MathFormula>

Water: ~1500 m/s

Steel: ~5000 m/s

# 6. Pressure–Velocity Relationship in Plane Waves

Using Euler’s equation:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\rho_0 \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial p'}{\partial x}`}</MathFormula>

For a plane wave:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`u(x,t) = \frac{p'(x,t)}{\rho_0 c}`}</MathFormula>

This leads to the characteristic impedance:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`Z_0 = \rho_0 c`}</MathFormula>

This relation is crucial in: Absorption, Boundary reflection, Room acoustics, Loudspeaker radiation and Duct acoustics.

Pressure and velocity are in phase in a plane wave — a key simplification.

---

# 7. Acoustic Intensity

Acoustic intensity expresses rate of sound energy transport:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`I(x,t) = p'(x,t) u(x,t)`}</MathFormula>

For a plane wave:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\overline{I} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}{\rho_0 c}`}</MathFormula>

This formula underpins: sound power calculations, directional radiation, energy-based room models, level and decibel calculations.

--- 

# 8. Plane Waves and the Helmholtz Equation

Time-harmonic acoustics eliminates explicit time dependence:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`p'(x,t) = \Re\{ \tilde{P} e^{j\omega t} \}`}</MathFormula>

Substituting into the wave equation gives:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`\nabla^2 \tilde{P} + k^2 \tilde{P} = 0`}</MathFormula>

This Helmholtz equation is the fundamental PDE of:

room acoustics

duct acoustics

scattering

absorption modeling

simulation (FEM/BEM/FDTD frequency domain)

Plane waves are its canonical simplest solution.

---

# 9. Oblique Incidence & Angle of Propagation

In 2D:

<MathFormula>{String.raw`p'(x,y,t) = A \cos(\omega t - k(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta))`}</MathFormula>

Angle <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\theta`}</MathFormula> determines:

how waves reflect

how absorption varies

how boundary conditions behave

Why angle matters:

Real reflections depend on the angle of incidence.

Many wall materials absorb differently at different angles.

Specular reflection laws derive from plane-wave incidence.

This is the foundation of:

    <li>ray tracing</li>
    <li>boundary impedance models</li>
    <li>scattering equations</li>
  </ul>
</div>

# 13. Applications in Real Acoustics

Plane waves are not abstract mathematical tricks — they form the foundation of EVERYTHING in acoustics.

### ✔ Far-field of loudspeakers  
Where wavefront curvature is small and approximates a plane.

### ✔ Room acoustics and reflections  
Each reflection can be modeled as a plane wave.

### ✔ Impulse responses  
High-frequency early reflections behave like plane waves.

### ✔ Binaural rendering (HRTFs)  
Plane waves form the canonical “incoming direction” model.

### ✔ Duct acoustics  
Below the cutoff frequency, only the plane-wave mode propagates.

### ✔ Absorption & boundary modeling  
Reflection and absorption formulas assume plane-wave incidence.

### ✔ Mode analysis  
Modes can be expressed as sums of plane waves.

<ul className="plane-waves-summary-list">
  <li>Plane waves are exact solutions of the acoustic wave equation.</li>
  <li>They have constant-phase wavefronts and travel in the direction of <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\mathbf{k}`}</MathFormula>.</li>
  <li>Pressure and velocity are simply related by <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`u = \frac{p'}{\rho_0 c}`}</MathFormula>.</li>
  <li>Acoustic intensity is expressible directly as <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\overline{I} = p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/(\rho_0 c)`}</MathFormula>.</li>
  <li>The speed of sound emerges from thermodynamic or elastic properties.</li>
  <li>The Helmholtz equation governs plane-wave spatial fields.</li>
  <li>Angle of incidence controls reflection and absorption.</li>
  <li>Plane waves underpin most of acoustic modeling, simulation, and rendering.</li>
</ul>
