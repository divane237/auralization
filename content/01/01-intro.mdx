## 1. What Is Sound?

Sound begins when a source vibrates and displaces the surrounding medium. Each push/pull slightly increases or decreases the local pressure and that change ripples outward.

<Callout type="success" title="Key Idea">
  Particles barely move from their resting positions—the **energy pattern** is
  what travels. Think of a stadium wave: people stand up and sit down, yet the
  wave circles the arena.
</Callout>

### 1.1 Pressure Variations

The disturbance alternates between:

- **Compression** → molecules squeeze together → pressure spikes above ambient.
- **Rarefaction** → molecules spread apart → pressure dips below ambient.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter01/subchp01-1.1-1.jpeg"
  caption="Compression and rarefaction regions radiating away from a source"
/>

These oscillations create the wavefront that eventually reaches your ear or a microphone capsule.

### 1.2 A Quick Checklist

- Source pushes nearby molecules.
- Neighboring molecules collide and transfer momentum.
- The pressure disturbance propagates outward.
- A receiver senses that variation and converts it to an electrical or neural signal.

## 2. Sound as a Longitudinal Wave

Air is a fluid, so sound propagates as a **longitudinal wave**:

- Particle motion is **parallel** to the direction of propagation
- Pressure variations move forward, but particles only oscillate locally

Difference from transverse waves:

  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr>
        <th>Property</th>
        <th>Longitudinal Wave (Sound in Air)</th>
        <th>Transverse Wave (String)</th>
      </tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <td>Particle motion</td>
        <td>Parallel to travel direction</td>
        <td>Perpendicular</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td>Needs a medium?</td>
        <td>Yes (fluid or solid)</td>
        <td>Typically solids/strings</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td>Restoring force</td>
        <td>Pressure &amp; compressibility</td>
        <td>Tension or shear</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch Out">
  In popular diagrams the wave often looks sinusoidal, but the drawing
  represents **pressure over distance**, not literal particle paths.
</Callout>

## 3. Time-Domain Representation

We usually plot sound as pressure (y-axis) versus time (x-axis). Smooth shapes imply periodic content; jagged shapes imply transients or noise.

<TimeDomainVisualizer />

### 3.1 Idealized Sine Wave

<MathFormula>{`p(t) = A \\sin(2\\pi f t + \\phi)`}</MathFormula>

- **A** controls amplitude (loudness perception).
- **f** sets the oscillation rate (pitch).
- **φ** shifts the waveform in time (phase).

<WaveformViewer frequency={330} amplitude={0.8} phase={Math.PI / 6} showGrid />

A sine wave produces the simplest, smoothest oscillation. This is the foundation of tones, Fourier analysis, filters, sound synthesis

### 3.2 Real-World Waveforms

Speech, music, and noise look complex and irregular, unlike sine waves.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter01/subchp01-3.2-1.png"
  caption="Speech and noise snippets in the time domain"
/>

- Speech shows quick transients and pauses.
- Sustained musical notes exhibit repeating envelopes plus harmonics.
- Noise occupies many frequencies simultaneously, so the waveform looks erratic.

## 4. Core Acoustic Quantities

Let’s break down the foundational physical quantities that describe sound.

### 4.1 Frequency (Pitch)

f = number of oscillations per second (Hz)

  <table>

      </tr>

        <td>Upper hearing</td>
        <td>~20,000</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>

Higher frequency → higher pitch and Lower frequency → lower pitch

### 4.2 Amplitude (Loudness)

<Callout type="danger" title="Amplitude ≠ Loudness">
  Amplitude is a physical pressure difference. Loudness is psychoacoustic—it
  depends on **amplitude** and **frequency**. For example, 40 dB SPL at 100 Hz
  feels quieter than 40 dB SPL at 2 kHz.
</Callout>

### 4.3 Wavelength

<MathFormula>{`\\lambda = \\frac{c}{f}`}</MathFormula>

  <table>

Short wavelengths reflect off small surfaces; long wavelengths diffract around obstacles and excite room modes.

### 4.4 Speed of Sound

<MathFormula>
  {`c \\approx 331 + 0.6T \\quad \\text{(m/s, with T in °C)}`}
</MathFormula>

At 20 °C → **343 m/s**. Temperature dominates, but humidity and the medium itself matter too. Sound travels fastest in stiff, dense media such as steel.

## 6. Sound in Different Media

<div className="table-wrapper">
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr>
        <th>Medium</th>
        <th>Speed of Sound</th>
        <th>Behavior Notes</th>
      </tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <td>Air</td>
        <td>~343 m/s</td>
        <td>Low density, strong temperature effect</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td>Water</td>
        <td>~1480 m/s</td>
        <td>Higher pressure, sound travels farther</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td>Steel</td>
        <td>~5960 m/s</td>
        <td>Extremely stiff, minimal attenuation</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The generic relationship is:

<MathFormula>{`c = \\sqrt{\\frac{K}{\\rho}}`}</MathFormula>

Where **K** is the bulk modulus (stiffness) and **ρ** is density. Stiffer media push sound along faster.

- **Pitch** corresponds to frequency (with musical context layered on top).
- **Loudness** depends on both amplitude and frequency, described by equal-loudness contours.
- **Timbre** is shaped by harmonic structure and transients.
- **Localization** leverages timing and level differences between our ears.

## 10. Summary Checklist

- Sound is a **mechanical, longitudinal wave** that requires a medium.
- Pressure variations, not mass transport, carry the energy.
- Waveforms plot pressure vs. time; sine waves are the simplest building blocks.
- Frequency, amplitude, wavelength, and speed of sound describe physical behavior.
- Human perception maps those quantities to pitch, loudness, timbre, and spatial cues.
- Simple home experiments reinforce how sound waves behave in everyday settings.
