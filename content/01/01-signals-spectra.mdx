<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>1. Introduction: Why Do We Need Spectra?</h2>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Up to now, we have viewed sound through two lenses:
    pressure fluctuations in <strong>time</strong> and waves propagating in <strong>space</strong>. Acoustics
    demands a third, equally powerful perspective:
    <strong>frequency</strong>.
  </div>
  <div className="spectra-pillars">
    <div className="spectra-pill">
      Pressure in <strong>time</strong> emphasizes envelopes, attack, and sustain.
    </div>
    <div className="spectra-pill">
      Plane waves in <strong>space</strong> show direction, energy flow, and boundary interactions.
    </div>
    <div className="spectra-pill">
      <strong>Frequency</strong> reveals pitch, timbre, and brightness — the qualities our ears latch on to.
    </div>
  </div>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Every complex sound decomposes into low, middle, and high frequencies; spectral analysis is the tool
    that makes that decomposition visible and measurable.
  </div>
  <div className="paragraph">
    This chapter explains what a spectrum is, how to interpret frequency content, and why it lies at the
    heart of acoustics and auralization.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>2. Time-Domain View: Signals as Functions of Time</h2>
  <div className="paragraph">
    The sound pressure waveform <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`p'(t)`}</MathFormula> traces pressure changes over time.
  </div>
  <ul className="section-list">
    <li>A sine wave → smooth, harmonic oscillation.</li>
    <li>A clap → sharp transient burst.</li>
    <li>Speech → structured yet non-periodic fluctuations.</li>
    <li>Music → layers of harmonic content and rhythmic motion.</li>
  </ul>
  <div className="paragraph">
    While the time-domain signal tells us how pressure evolves, it can obscure the internal structure.
    Two waveforms may look similar on a plot but sound drastically different; a spectral view reveals the
    ingredients behind the waveform.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>3. Frequency-Domain View: Signals as Sums of Sinusoids</h2>
  <div className="section-callout">
    <div className="callout-text">
      <strong>Any signal</strong> can be represented as a sum of sinusoids. That principle underpins the
      Fourier series and transform.
    </div>
  </div>

  <div className="paragraph">
    The Fourier series reconstructs <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`x(t)`}</MathFormula> from discrete spectral lines,
    so every waveform has a unique set of coefficients.
  </div>

  <h3>3.1 Fourier Series (Periodic Signals)</h3>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} C_n e^{\mathrm{i} n \omega_0 t}
  `}</MathFormula>

  <div className="paragraph">
    Periodic signals with period <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`T`}</MathFormula> produce discrete spectral lines.
  </div>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    C_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T x(t) e^{-\mathrm{i} n \omega_0 t} \, dt
  `}</MathFormula>

  <div className="paragraph">
    Writing <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`C_n = |C_n| e^{\mathrm{i} \phi_n}`}</MathFormula> shows that both amplitude
<MathFormula inline>{String.raw`|C_n|`}</MathFormula> and phase <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\phi_n`}</MathFormula> reside in the spectrum:
  </div>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |C_n| e^{\mathrm{i}(n \omega_0 t + \phi_n)}
  `}</MathFormula>

  <div className="paragraph">
    Here, <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\omega_0 = 2\pi/T`}</MathFormula> is the fundamental angular frequency, and
    <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`C_n`}</MathFormula> are the spectral lines that describe amplitude and phase.
  </div>

  <h3>3.2 Fourier Transform (Non-Periodic Signals)</h3>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Most natural sounds (speech, claps, noise) are non-periodic and rely on the continuous Fourier transform <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`X(\omega)`}</MathFormula>.
  </div>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    X(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-\mathrm{i} \omega t} \, dt
  `}</MathFormula>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(\omega) e^{\mathrm{i} \omega t} \, d\omega
  `}</MathFormula>

  <ul className="section-list">
    <li>
      <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`X(\omega)`}</MathFormula> is the complex spectrum.
    </li>
    <li>
      <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`|X(\omega)|`}</MathFormula> is the magnitude (how much energy at each frequency).
    </li>
    <li>
      <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`\arg(X(\omega))`}</MathFormula> is the phase (how sinusoids align in time).
    </li>
  </ul>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Sharp transients spread energy across frequencies; pure tones concentrate it narrowly; noise produces a
    broad, often smooth spectrum.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>4. Wideband Signals vs Narrowband Signals</h2>
  <div className="signal-type-grid">
    <div className="signal-type-card">
      <h3>Wideband Signals</h3>
      <div className="card-description">Contain many frequencies simultaneously.</div>
      <ul className="section-list">
        <li>Claps, consonants like “s” and “t”, explosions.</li>
        <li>Pink or white noise.</li>
        <li>Spectra: broad continuous distributions.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div className="signal-type-card">
      <h3>Narrowband Signals</h3>
      <div className="card-description">Dominated by one or few frequencies.</div>
      <ul className="section-list">
        <li>Pure sine tones, tuning forks.</li>
        <li>Steady musical notes and vowel sounds.</li>
        <li>Spectra: sharp peaks or harmonic lines.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div className="paragraph">
    These distinctions influence localization, perception, reverberation, speech intelligibility, and filter
    design. Understanding what dominates a signal’s spectrum helps us process or recreate it correctly.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>5. Spectrum Interpretation</h2>
  <div className="paragraph">
    The magnitude spectrum reveals where the energy is concentrated and what frequency regions define timbre.
  </div>
  <div className="paragraph">
    The phase spectrum controls waveform shape, transients, and localization cues; omitting it hampers perfect reconstruction.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>6. Time–Frequency Trade-Off: Why We Need Spectrograms</h2>
  <div className="paragraph">
    A spectrum tells us “what” frequencies exist but not “when” they occur. Speech and music evolve rapidly, so we
    need time–frequency tools like the short-time Fourier transform (STFT):
  </div>
  <MathFormula>{String.raw`
    X(t, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) w(t - \tau) e^{-\mathrm{i} \omega \tau} \, d\tau
  `}</MathFormula>

  <div className="paragraph">
    Here,
    <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`w(t)`}</MathFormula> is a window function (Hann, Hamming, etc.), and
    <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`X(t, \omega)`}</MathFormula> becomes the time–frequency representation.
  </div>
  <div className="spectrogram-callout">
    <div className="callout-text">
      The spectrogram <MathFormula inline>{String.raw`S(t,\omega)`}</MathFormula> is the squared magnitude:
    </div>
    <MathFormula>{String.raw`S(t,\omega) = |X(t,\omega)|^2`}</MathFormula>
    <div className="callout-text">It shows how energy migrates across frequency bands over time.</div>
  </div>
  <h3>6.1 Time–Frequency Resolution</h3>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Short windows give sharp time details but blur frequency; long windows reveal frequency finely but smear time.
  </div>
  <div className="paragraph">
    The uncertainty principle makes this trade-off unavoidable. Speech favors short windows; steady music can afford longer ones.
  </div>
</section>

<section className="chapter-section">
  <h2>11. Python Demonstration: Compute FFT &amp; Plot Spectrum</h2>
  <div className="paragraph">
    Use the interactive lab below to run the FFT example, tweak the signal, and inspect how the plotted
    spectrum responds in real time.
  </div>
  <PythonDemo
    id="signals-spectrum-fft"
    title="Spectrum of a Mixed Tone"
    description="Generate a mix of 440 Hz and 1 kHz tones, run the FFT, and display the magnitude spectrum."
    defaultCode={String.raw`
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# sampling parameters
fs = 48000
T = 1.0
t = np.linspace(0, T, int(fs*T), endpoint=False)

# signal: mix of 440 Hz + 1000 Hz
x = 0.7*np.sin(2*np.pi*440*t) + 0.3*np.sin(2*np.pi*1000*t)

# FFT
X = np.fft.rfft(x)
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1/fs)

plt.plot(freqs, 20*np.log10(np.abs(X)+1e-12))
plt.xlabel("Frequency (Hz)")
plt.ylabel("Magnitude (dB)")
plt.title("Spectrum of Mixed Tone")
plt.grid(True)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
    `}
