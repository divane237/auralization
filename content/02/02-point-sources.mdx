## 1. Why We Start with Point Sources

When studying sound radiation, real sources can be geometrically complex: loudspeakers have finite size, machines have multiple vibrating parts, and musical instruments have intricate shapes.
Analyzing such sources directly would be extremely difficult.

To make progress, acoustics begins with an idealized source model: the point source.

<Callout type="success" title="Key idea">
  A point source is not meant to represent a real object exactly. Instead, it serves as a reference model that captures the essential physics of sound radiation while remaining mathematically tractable. Many real sources behave approximately like point sources when observed from sufficiently far away.

</Callout>

---

## 2. The Point Source Concept

A point source is an idealized emitter that:

- radiates sound equally in every direction

- has a physical size negligible compared to the radiated wavelength

- sends its acoustic energy uniformly into the surrounding space

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter02/subchapt01-1.1.png"
  alt="Spherical wavefronts from a point source"
  caption="Snapshots of spherical wavefronts marching outward from a point source."
/>

Each circular wavefront marks a surface of equal phase, and as the source keeps pushing, the spheres grow larger just like ripples on a pond.
This geometric spreading is the reason the intensity drops with distance.

---

## 3. Spherical Wave Propagation

Unlike plane waves, which carry energy across flat wavefronts, a point source creates spherical wavefronts that expand outward.
As the radius grows, the same amount of acoustic power is shared among increasingly larger surface areas, so the intensity must fall.

The surface area of a sphere grows as:

<MathFormula> {"A = 4\\pi r^2"} </MathFormula>

Because power is conserved, the intensity decreases proportionally to the reciprocal of that surface. The inverse-square relationship follows directly.

---

## 4. Inverse-Square Law

The sound intensity at a distance \( r \) from a point source is:

<MathFormula> {"I(r) = \\frac{W}{4\\pi r^2}"} </MathFormula>

where:

𝑊 is the source power

𝑟 is the distance from the source

This equation shows how the same energy is diluted over a growing spherical shell, so intensity drops rapidly as you step away.

<Callout type="warning" title="Why −6 dB matters">
  Every time you double the distance from a point source, the sound pressure
  level falls by roughly 6 dB. That rule of thumb comes right out of the
  inverse-square law.
</Callout>

---

## 5. Distance Dependence of Sound Pressure

Sound pressure is proportional to the square root of intensity:

<MathFormula> {"I \\propto p_{\\mathrm{rms}}^2"} </MathFormula>

Combining this with the inverse-square law gives:

<MathFormula> {"p_{\\mathrm{rms}}(r) \\propto \\frac{1}{r}"} </MathFormula>

Doubling the distance halves the pressure amplitude, so amplitude-based measurements also reflect the −6 dB rule.
When expressed in decibels, the falloff becomes even easier to handle in logarithmic equations.

---

## 6. Sound Pressure Level vs Distance

The difference in SPL between two distances <MathFormula inline> {"{r_1}"} </MathFormula> and <MathFormula inline> {"{r_2}"} </MathFormula> is:

<MathFormula>
  {" "}
  {"\\Delta L_p = 20\\log_{10}\\left(\\frac{r_1}{r_2}\\right)"}{" "}
</MathFormula>

A special and practical case is distance doubling:

<MathFormula> {"\\Delta L_p = -6\\,\\mathrm{dB}"} </MathFormula>

This is the livelihood of outdoor sound prediction, microphone calibration, and any design effort that must relate a source to a listener at a known range.

---

## 7. Near Field and Far Field

The spherical-wave approximation works best in the far field, where the distance is large compared to the source size, wavefronts look smooth, and pressure and particle velocity stay in phase.
Closer to the source we find the near field, where reactive energy, phase mismatches, and rapid amplitude changes dominate.

- Far field → clean inverse-distance relationships

- Near field → reactive energy, phase shifts, and measurement challenges

Understanding the transition matters because measurements taken inside the near field can be misleading if you treat them like far-field readings.

<Callout type="warning" title="Measurement note">
  If your microphone sits too close to a source, you will sense reactive fields
  that do not translate to perceived loudness far away. Design measurements with
  distance in mind.
</Callout>

## 9. Python Demonstration: Distance Attenuation

Use the sandboxed Python lab below to compute how the SPL falls off with increasing distance from a point source.

<div className="python-demo">
  <PythonDemo
    id="point-sources-attenuation"
    title="Point-source SPL decay"
    description="Calculate SPL at multiple distances and observe the −6 dB drop when doubling the range."
    defaultCode={String.raw`
import numpy as np

# reference values

Lp_ref = 90 # SPL at reference distance (dB)
r_ref = 1.0 # reference distance (m)

# distances to evaluate

r = np.array([1, 2, 4, 8])

# SPL as function of distance

Lp = Lp_ref - 20 \* np.log10(r / r_ref)

for dist, level in zip(r, Lp):
print(f"Distance: {dist} m → SPL: {level:.1f} dB")
`}
/>

</div>

This numerical example confirms the −6 dB rule for each doubling of distance.

## 10. Practical Implications

The point source and spherical wave model guide many real-world applications:

- Environmental noise prediction

- Outdoor sound propagation models

- Reference sound power measurements

- Initial room acoustics estimates

Even if sources are not perfect points, this framework remains powerful because more complex models ultimately build on these simple relations.

---

## 11. Summary

A point source radiates evenly in every direction, producing spherical waves whose energy spreads over growing surface areas.
The inverse-square law and the −6 dB per doubling rule follow quickly, and these principles support later discussions about directivity and acoustic measurements.
