## 1. Why Source Directivity Matters

When a sound source emits energy, that energy does not necessarily spread evenly in all directions. Real devices such as loudspeakers,
machines, and voices shape their radiation through geometry, baffles, and operating mechanisms. A listener who stands in front of a loudspeaker
hears more energy than one standing behind it, even when both observers are the same distance away.

Understanding directivity is essential because it drives:

* How sound propagates in rooms and interacts with surfaces
* How noise spreads into the environment and exposes nearby listeners
* How measurements depend on microphone position and orientation
* How listeners perceive loudness and clarity depending on where they sit

<Callout type="warning" title="Listener direction matters">
  Every ray of sound carries different energy. Directivity patterns guide decisions about microphone placement, seat layout,
  and absorptive treatments long before gain structure is even set.
</Callout>

---

## 2. Omnidirectional vs. Directional Sources

An omnidirectional source radiates sound equally in all directions.
In practice, true omnidirectional sources do not exist, but some sources approximate this behavior at low frequencies or over limited frequency ranges.

A directional source, on the other hand, emits sound preferentially in certain directions. This behavior becomes more pronounced as:

  the source size becomes comparable to the wavelength

  frequency increases

  the source geometry becomes asymmetric

At low frequencies, where wavelengths are long, many sources behave almost omnidirectionally. As frequency increases, interference and diffraction effects cause increasingly complex radiation patterns.

## 3. Describing Directivity Quantitatively

While visual patterns are helpful, acoustics requires quantitative measures of directionality.
To achieve this, we compare the sound intensity radiated in a given direction to the intensity that would be produced by an ideal omnidirectional source radiating the same total sound power.

This comparison leads to the concept of the directivity factor.

---

## 4. Directivity Factor

The directivity factor, denoted by 𝑄, is defined as the ratio between:

<ul className="mt-4 list-disc space-y-2 pl-5 text-base text-gray-800 dark:text-slate-100">
  <li>the sound intensity in a given direction, and</li>
  <li>the average sound intensity over all directions</li>
</ul>

Mathematically, it is expressed as:
<MathFormula>{"Q = \\frac{4\\pi I(\\theta,\\phi)}{\\int_{4\\pi} I(\\theta,\\phi)\\, d\\Omega}"}</MathFormula>

This definition reflects an important idea:
If a source radiates more energy in a particular direction than the average, the directivity factor in that direction is greater than one.

Special cases help build intuition:

* Omnidirectional source: <MathFormula inline>{"Q = 1"}</MathFormula>

* Hemispherical radiation (e.g., source near a rigid plane): <MathFormula inline>{"Q \\approx 2"}</MathFormula>

* Highly focused sources: <MathFormula inline>{"Q \\geq 1"}</MathFormula>

The directivity factor therefore tells us how concentrated the radiation is in a given direction.


---

## 5. Directivity Index

Because acoustics usually works in decibels, we often convert the directivity factor to the directivity index (DI):

<MathFormula>{"\\mathrm{DI} = 10\\log_{10}(Q)"}</MathFormula>

The DI expresses how much louder a source appears in a specific direction compared to an omnidirectional reference source with the same power output.
Typical values include:

* Omnidirectional source: <MathFormula inline>{"\\mathrm{DI} = 10\\log_{10}(1) = 0\\,\\mathrm{dB}"}</MathFormula>

* Source radiating into a half-space: <MathFormula inline>{"\\mathrm{DI} = 10\\log_{10}(2) \\approx 3.01\\,\\mathrm{dB}"}</MathFormula>

* Highly directional loudspeaker (e.g. <MathFormula inline>{"Q = 4"}</MathFormula>): <MathFormula inline>{"\\mathrm{DI} = 10\\log_{10}(4) \\approx 6.02\\,\\mathrm{dB}"}</MathFormula>

The logarithmic form makes directivity indices easy to compare and integrate into level-based calculations.


---

## 6. Physical Interpretation of Directivity

Directivity arises from wave interference and diffraction.
When sound waves emitted from different parts of a source combine, they may reinforce or cancel each other depending on direction and frequency.

Key factors influencing directivity include:

<ul className="mt-4 list-disc space-y-2 pl-5 text-base text-gray-800 dark:text-slate-100">
  <li>source size relative to wavelength</li>
  <li>source geometry</li>
  <li>boundary conditions (e.g., mounting near walls or floors)</li>
  <li>frequency</li>
</ul>

As frequency increases, wavelengths become shorter, and interference effects become more pronounced. This is why loudspeakers and musical instruments typically become more directional at higher frequencies.

## 8. Applications of Directivity

Source directivity informs many real-world tasks:

* Room acoustics design (early reflections, clarity, spatial impression)
* Environmental noise control (uneven exposure depending on source orientation)
* Loudspeaker design (controlled coverage and reduced unwanted reflections)
* Acoustic measurements (results shift with microphone direction)

<Callout type="warning" title="Measurement sensitivity">
  Measurements taken without accounting for directivity risk large errors. Microphone position and orientation should match the intended listening direction.
</Callout>

---

## 9. Summary

Sources rarely radiate uniformly. Directivity factors and indices quantify how directional a source is, giving designers and engineers the tools
they need to predict propagation, perception, and measurement outcomes.
