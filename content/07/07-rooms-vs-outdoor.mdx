## 1. Why Environment Matters in Acoustic Simulation

Sound follows the same governing equations everywhere, yet the dominant phenomena shift dramatically depending on boundaries, medium homogeneity, and the distance scales involved.

<Callout type="success" title="Key idea">
The physics that dominate—reflections, diffraction, elastic waves—depend on the geometry and materials of your problem, so your numerical strategy must follow suit rather than assume a one-size-fits-all solver.
</Callout>

### 1.1 Scenario-dependent physics

- Presence or absence of enclosing boundaries changes how energy is retained or leaks.  
- The propagation medium (air, water, solids) alters speed, attenuation, and whether longitudinal or elastic waves dominate.  
- Distance scales determine whether geometrical spreading, diffraction, or reverberant buildup is the key concern.  
- Coupling between air and solids shifts focus to interface impedance rather than pure air propagation.

### 1.2 Consequences for simulation

Keeping all phenomena in a single model quickly becomes inefficient. Instead, identify the subset you cannot ignore for each context and adopt methods that capture those effects without overcomplicating the rest.

---

## 2. Room Acoustics Simulation

Room acoustics targets enclosed or semi-enclosed volumes—concert halls, classrooms, cabins—where walls and ceilings shape the entire field.

### 2.1 Dominant physical phenomena

- Multiple reflections on walls, floor, and ceiling.  
- Standing waves and room modes that dominate the low-frequency response.  
- Diffuse reverberant fields emerging at mid and high frequencies.  
- Spatial variations that make impulse responses and pressure maps highly location-dependent.

### 2.2 Typical simulation approaches

- **Wave-based solvers** (finite element, boundary element) for modal regions.  
- **Geometrical acoustics** (ray tracing, beam tracing) for mid/high frequencies.  
- **Hybrid methods** to stitch modal accuracy with ray-based coverage for broadband responses.

<Callout type="warning" title="Boundary reminder">
Strong boundaries trap and fold energy, so even small geometry changes can shift modal patterns dramatically.

## 3. Outdoor Acoustics Simulation

Outdoor acoustics concerns open urban plazas, streets, and transportation corridors where enclosing walls fade and atmospheric effects grow.

### 3.1 Dominant physical phenomena

- Spherical or cylindrical spreading that drives rapid level decay.  
- Ground reflections and impedance that bias energy toward the receiver.  
- Diffraction around buildings, barriers, and terrain features.  
- Atmospheric absorption and refraction from wind or temperature gradients.

### 3.2 Typical simulation approaches

- Ray-based geometrical acoustics enhanced with diffraction corrections.  
- Parabolic equation methods for long-range propagation where curvature matters.  
- Statistical or engineering noise models for rapid compliance assessments.

## 4. Structure-Borne Acoustics Simulation

Structure-borne acoustics follows elastic waves inside solids, including how vibrating walls, floors, or machinery excite the surrounding air.

### 4.1 Dominant physical phenomena

- Elastic wave propagation (longitudinal, shear, flexural) through beams, plates, or shells.  
- Resonances of stiff elements (panels, shells) that store and re-radiate energy.  
- Coupling losses when structural motion leaks into air entrances.

### 4.2 Typical simulation approaches

- Finite element models (FEM) for the structural domain.  
- Boundary element methods (BEM) to capture radiation into air.  
- Coupled vibro-acoustic simulations that exchange boundary conditions between solids and fluids.

## 5. Coupling Between Domains

Real systems rarely stay confined to a single domain. Loudspeakers shake enclosures, machinery shakes floors, and façades radiate back into the street.

- Vibrating walls launch airborne pressure waves.  
- Machinery excitation transmits into floors and ceilings.  
- Façade radiation blends interior and exterior fields.

<Callout type="success" title="Close the loop">
Coupling often forces you to solve two domains simultaneously or iterate between solvers to honor boundary reciprocity.
</Callout>

---

## 6. Choosing the Right Simulation Strategy

Picking a method comes down to comparing the dominant effects in each environment with the solver’s strengths.

<div className="table-wrapper"><table><thead><tr><th>Environment</th><th>Dominant Effects</th><th>Typical Methods</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Rooms</td><td>Reflections, modes, reverberation</td><td>Wave-based, geometrical, hybrid</td></tr><tr><td>Outdoor</td><td>Spreading, diffraction, atmosphere</td><td>Geometrical, parabolic equation, engineering models</td></tr><tr><td>Structure-borne</td><td>Elastic waves, vibration</td><td>FEM, BEM, vibro-acoustic coupling</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

Combining domain awareness with solver capabilities keeps simulations accurate without wasting compute on negligible effects.

---

## 7. Practical Implications

Misapplying a method leads to flawed level predictions, misleading spatial maps, and expensive design decisions based on the wrong physics.

<Callout type="success" title="Why it matters">
- A room-mode solver cannot safely predict outdoor propagation because it ignores diffraction and spreading.  
- A ray-based outdoor solver misses the discrete modes that dominate small enclosed spaces.  
- Ignoring structure-borne paths underestimates vibration transfer and radiated noise.
</Callout>

---

## 8. Summary Checklist

- Rooms trap energy with strong boundary reflections and modes.  
- Outdoor domains dilute energy via spreading and diffraction, so large-scale efficiency matters.  
- Structure-borne paths travel inside solids and must couple to air carefully.  
- Coupled simulations ensure reciprocity between domains.
