## 1. From Mono to Spatial Audio

Early audio reproduction systems were **monophonic**, delivering the same signal to every listener. While the content is preserved, position cues vanish.

<Callout type="success" title="Think Spatially">
Spatial audio swaps uniform loudness for directional cues: encode **where** a sound is located, not just **what** it is.
</Callout>

Spatial audio systems encode location through intensity, timing, or directional representations and then render that information through multiple loudspeakers or headphones.

Three major paradigms dominate modern spatial audio:

- Stereo (two-channel reproduction)  
- Surround sound (multi-channel loudspeaker layouts)  
- Ambisonics (sound field–based representation)  

Each paradigm reflects a different approach to representing acoustic space.

---

## 2. Stereo Sound Reproduction

Stereo systems use **two loudspeakers**, typically positioned symmetrically in front of the listener. Spatial perception arises primarily through **interaural level differences** created by amplitude panning.

### 2.1 Phantom Source Formation

When identical signals are played from both loudspeakers at equal level, the listener perceives a **phantom source** centered between them. Adjusting relative levels shifts the phantom source laterally.

This technique is known as **amplitude panning**.

<Callout type="info" title="Phantom Sources">
By balancing levels you can “steer” the phantom image, but listener position and room acoustics limit how consistent that feeling remains.
</Callout>

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter06/subchapter01-2.1.1.png"
  caption="Stereo phantom source illustrations explain how amplitude panning shifts localization."
/>

### 2.2 Properties of Stereo

Stereo reproduction:
- provides stable left–right localization  
- depends strongly on listener position  
- does not reproduce elevation or depth accurately  

Stereo remains widely used due to its simplicity, compatibility, and efficiency, but it is fundamentally limited in spatial realism.

---

## 3. Surround Sound Systems

Surround sound extends stereo by introducing **additional loudspeakers** placed around the listener. Common layouts include:

- 5.1 surround  
- 7.1 surround  
- extended cinema and immersive layouts  

### 3.1 Channel-Based Encoding

In surround sound, each channel corresponds to a **specific loudspeaker**. Sound designers explicitly route signals to channels to create spatial effects.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter06/subchapter01-3.1.png"
  caption="Channel-based encoding routes signals to specific loudspeakers for surround layouts."
/>

### 3.2 Characteristics of Surround Sound

Surround systems:
- improve envelopment and immersion  
- allow discrete placement of sound sources  
- are tied to specific loudspeaker layouts  

While effective in cinemas and home theaters, surround sound systems lack flexibility when listener position or loudspeaker geometry changes.

---

## 4. Limitations of Channel-Based Spatial Audio

Both stereo and surround sound share a key limitation: they encode spatial information **relative to loudspeakers**, not relative to the listener or the sound scene.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch Out">
If the listener moves or the loudspeaker positions change, the encoded scene breaks, leading to spatial distortion or loss of envelopment.
</Callout>

This approach causes:
- spatial distortion when the listener moves  
- limited scalability to different playback setups  
- difficulty representing continuous sound fields  

These limitations motivate alternative representations of spatial audio.

---

## 5. Ambisonics: A Sound Field Representation

Ambisonics takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of encoding signals for specific loudspeakers, it represents the **entire sound field** around a listener.

The sound field is decomposed into a set of **spherical harmonic components**.

### 5.1 First-Order Ambisonics (FOA)

In first-order Ambisonics, the sound field is represented by four channels:

- <MathFormula inline>{"(W)"}</MathFormula>: omnidirectional component  
- <MathFormula inline>{"(X, Y, Z)"}</MathFormula>: directional components  

<MathFormula>
{"\\mathbf{B} = [W, X, Y, Z]"}
</MathFormula>

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter06/subchapter01-5.1.png"
  caption="FOA channels capture omnidirectional energy plus three directional components."
/>

These components describe how sound energy is distributed in space.

---

## 6. Encoding and Decoding in Ambisonics

### 6.1 Encoding

Sound sources are encoded into the Ambisonic domain based on their direction. This process remains independent of the playback system.

### 6.2 Decoding

During playback, the Ambisonic representation is decoded to:
- a loudspeaker array  
- binaural signals for headphones  

Decoding adapts the sound field to the available reproduction system.

---

## 7. Higher-Order Ambisonics

Higher-order Ambisonics (HOA) increases spatial resolution by using additional spherical harmonic components.

Key properties:
- higher order → sharper spatial localization  
- increased computational cost  
- improved robustness to listener movement  

Ambisonics order determines how accurately the sound field is reconstructed.

## 9. Role in Virtual Acoustics

In virtual acoustics and VR systems:
- stereo is often insufficient  
- surround systems are impractical for mobile setups  
- Ambisonics provides a flexible, device-independent solution  

Ambisonics is therefore widely used as an intermediate format in modern 3D audio pipelines.

---

## 10. Summary

Stereo, surround, and Ambisonics represent successive stages in the evolution of spatial audio reproduction. Stereo relies on phantom imaging between two loudspeakers, surround sound extends spatial coverage using multiple fixed channels, and Ambisonics models the sound field itself. Among these approaches, Ambisonics offers the greatest flexibility and scalability for virtual acoustics and immersive audio systems.
