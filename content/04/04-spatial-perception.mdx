## 1. From Localization to Spatial Perception

Localization answers “Where is the sound?” Spatial perception answers questions about distance, width, envelopment, and whether a signal feels internal or external.

<Callout type="success" title="Key Idea">
Spatial perception is an emergent property: localization cues combine with spectral shaping, reflections, and context to paint a believable spatial image.
</Callout>

- The same azimuth can feel near or far depending on how loud the direct sound is and how many reflections arrive.
- Spectral richness, motion, and context (room size, object expectations) determine whether a source feels inside the head or outside it.
- Spatial perception is what makes a recorded rehearsal sound alive instead of flat.

## 2. Distance Perception

Distance is **never encoded by a single cue**—it is inferred from how the direct and reflected components of a sound interact.

<Callout type="success" title="Distance depends on multiple cues">
Our brains monitor level, spectral balance, and the relationship between direct and reverberant energy to judge how far away a source is.
</Callout>

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter04/subchapter04-2.1.jpg"
  caption="Direct sound decays quickly while reverberant energy persists, so the direct-to-reverberant ratio (DRR) shifts with distance."
/>

- **Sound level** drops with distance, but source power can disguise this cue.
- **Direct-to-reverberant ratio (DRR)** shrinks as the listener moves farther from the source.
- **High-frequency attenuation** grows because air absorption and surface reflections roll off the treble.
- **Temporal structure** (early reflections, onset build-up) also signals how the wavefront evolved.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch Out">
Level cues can be misleading if a powerful nearby source and a quiet distant source have similar levels; pairing level with DRR and spectral cues is safer.
</Callout>

## 3. Externalization

If a signal lacks realistic spatial cues, it collapses and sounds “inside the head.” Externalization succeeds when the cues point outward and stay consistent with the room.

- Accurate **interaural timing (ITD)** and **level (ILD)** cues anchor the azimuth.
- **Spectral shaping** (pinna and head filtering) encodes elevation and front/back.
- **Early reflections** and the precedence effect make the source feel fused with the room.
- Inconsistencies between the expected room and the delivered reflections will pull the source inside the head again.

## 4. Role of Early Reflections

Early reflections that arrive within roughly 50 ms reinforce both distance and externalization without breaking the direct sound’s dominance.

- They “paint” the room’s size and hardness while staying fused to the direct path.
- They support externalization by adding spatial context without sounding like discrete echoes.
- The **precedence effect** keeps later arrivals from shifting perceived direction.

## 5. Apparent Source Width (ASW)

ASW is how wide a source feels—one speaker can sound point-like or as broad as a barricade.

- Low interaural correlation (IACC) and **early lateral reflections** spread the image.
- Dense, diffuse reflections exaggerate width without moving the source direction.
- Source bandwidth and motion also influence perceived width: broader spectra feel wider.

## 6. Listener Envelopment (LEV)

LEV measures how much late energy surrounds the listener. It depends on the diffuse reflections that arrive from many directions.

- Late reverberation after ~80 ms fills in the tails of the impulse response.
- Diffuse energy coming from above and behind energizes the “surround” of the listener.
- Rooms with longer decay and richer late energy feel more enveloping.

## 7. Spatial Perception in Auralization

A convincing auralization reproduces distance, width, and envelopment while keeping the source externalized.

- Match **HRTFs** to the listener and integrate **room impulse responses (RIRs)** that capture both early and late energy.
- Blend **reflection modeling** with direct sound so the DRR and timing stay consistent as the listener moves.
- Use **head tracking** or subtle motion cues to keep the perception stable across orientations.

## 8. Summary

- Spatial perception layers distance, width, and envelopment on top of localization.
- Distance relies on level, DRR, spectral tilt, and reflection timing.
- Externalization succeeds when binaural cues, reflections, and spectral shaping all agree.
- Early reflections anchor the room, while ASW and LEV describe width and immersion.
- Convincing auralization requires careful tuning of HRTFs, reflections, and head tracking.
