## 1. From Sound Waves to Perception

Sound is a **physical phenomenon**: pressure variations propagate through the medium whenever a source vibrates. Hearing happens when those variations enter the ear and begin transforming into neural activity.

<Callout type="success" title="Key Idea">
  Psychoacoustics maps the acoustic stimulus to the auditory sensation—sometimes small physical differences feel huge, and very different stimuli can sound similar.
</Callout>

## 2. Anatomy of the Human Auditory System

The human ear routes the pressure wave through three regions:

- **Outer ear** (collects sound and shapes direction-dependent cues)  
- **Middle ear** (impedance matching between air and the inner-ear fluid)  
- **Inner ear** (frequency analysis and transduction)

## 3. Outer Ear: Sound Collection and Directional Filtering

The **outer ear** (pinna, auricle, and ear canal) grabs sound and imprints direction cues.

- collects incoming sound waves  
- provides **direction-dependent filtering**, especially at high frequencies  
- enhances front–back and elevation perception  

The ear canal resonates roughly between 2–4 kHz, amplifying the band that carries most speech content.

---

## 4. Middle Ear: Impedance Matching

The **middle ear** houses the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) that bridge air and the fluid-filled cochlea.

- Without impedance matching, most energy would bounce off the air–fluid boundary.  
- The ossicles provide mechanical leverage and area transformation to raise pressure at the oval window by ~20–30 dB.  
- The eustachian tube equalizes pressure with ambient air.

---

## 5. Inner Ear: Frequency Analysis and Transduction

The **inner ear** contains the cochlea, which converts vibration into electrical signals.

- Sound induces traveling waves along the basilar membrane.  
- High frequencies peak near the base; low frequencies peak near the apex.  
- This spatial ordering is called **tonotopy**.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter04/subchapter01-5.1.png"
  caption="Tonotopic organization of the cochlea along the basilar membrane."
/>

Hair cells transduce the membrane motion into neural firing sent to the auditory nerve.

---

## 6. Frequency vs Pitch

Frequency (Hz) is measurable; pitch is the perception that the brain constructs.

- Higher frequencies usually sound higher in pitch, but the mapping is nonlinear.  
- Complex tones can evoke a pitch even when the fundamental frequency is missing.  
- Pitch perception draws on place coding (where on the membrane the wave peaks) and temporal coding (neural timing).

## 8. Intensity vs Loudness

Sound intensity or sound pressure level is physical; loudness is subjective.

- Loudness does not increase linearly with intensity—+10 dB often feels like twice as loud.  
- Loudness depends heavily on frequency.  
- Psychoacoustic models use equal-loudness contours and frequency weighting (e.g., A-weighting) to describe perception.

---

## 9. Dynamic Range and Limits of Hearing

The auditory system handles an enormous dynamic range:

- Threshold of hearing: ~20 µPa  
- Threshold of pain: ~120–140 dB SPL  

<MathFormula>{"\\text{Dynamic Range} \\approx 10^{12}"}</MathFormula>

Adaptation helps the system stay functional across quiet and noisy environments.

---

## 10. Hearing Is an Active Process

Hearing constantly adjusts:

- adapts to background noise  
- emphasizes contrasts and transient changes  
- integrates timing and level across both ears

This active behavior primes us for the masking, localization, and spatial topics ahead.

---

## 11. Summary

- Hearing turns physical pressure waves into perceptual experiences.  
- The outer, middle, and inner ear each shape the path from stimulus to sensation.  
- The cochlea analyzes frequency via tonotopic mapping.  
- Pitch and loudness are perceptual layers built on measurable quantities.  
- Human hearing spans a broad frequency and level range while remaining adaptive.

These foundations support all subsequent psychoacoustic phenomena.
