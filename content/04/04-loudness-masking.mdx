## 1. From Sound Level to Loudness

In physical acoustics, strength is described with sound pressure, intensity, and SPL, but few of those numbers map directly to how loud a listener perceives the sound.

Loudness is a **perceptual attribute** that depends on the energy pattern the ear receives. Two sounds with the same SPL can feel equally loud or very different depending on their spectrum, bandwidth, and context.

<Callout type="success" title="Key Idea">
  Loudness reflects what the auditory system *feels*, so the same physical level can produce strikingly different sensations once frequency and masking are involved.
</Callout>

---

## 2. Loudness Is Frequency Dependent

Human hearing is not equally sensitive to every frequency. At moderate levels, the ear is most sensitive between roughly 2 and 5 kHz; very low and very high tones need more SPL to sound as loud.

- mid frequencies (≈ 2–5 kHz) are perceived as loudest  
- very low and very high frequencies require higher SPL to sound equally loud  

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch Out">
  Audibility does not track SPL: a 40 dB tone at 100 Hz will feel softer than a 40 dB tone near 2 kHz, so relying solely on level misrepresents the listener’s experience.
</Callout>

---

## 3. Equal-Loudness Contours

The link between SPL and loudness is captured by equal-loudness contours, which plot frequency against SPL for a given perceived loudness.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter04/subchapter02-3.1.png"
  caption="Each contour connects frequencies that sound equally loud at the labeled SPL."
/>

These contours flatten as level increases, low-frequency sensitivity improves with higher levels, and 1 kHz is the reference frequency for most measurements. They help explain why tone quality matters more than level alone.

<Callout type="info" title="Contour Insight">
  Equal-loudness surfaces remind us that loudness is frequency-shaped—low frequencies must climb to be heard at the same level as mid frequencies.
</Callout>

---

## 4. Loudness vs Loudness Level

It is important to distinguish between **loudness level (phons)** and **loudness (sones)**. Loudness level is a comparative scale referenced to a 1 kHz tone, while loudness uses a ratio scale that reflects how many times louder a sound feels relative to the reference.

- +10 phons ≈ twice the loudness  
- Loudness grows nonlinearly with SPL  

<MathFormula>{`\\text{Loudness (sones)} = 2^{\\frac{\\text{phons} - 40}{10}}`}</MathFormula>

<Callout type="info" title="Perceptual Scaling">
  Using phons and sones lets us compare perceptual steps even when SPL changes are ambiguous; 40 phons is defined as 1 sone, so doubling the loudness is approximately a 10-phon jump.
</Callout>

---

## 5. Bandwidth and Critical Bands

Loudness also depends on bandwidth. When bandwidth is smaller than a critical band, loudness increases rapidly with bandwidth; beyond that threshold, loudness grows more slowly.

- Bandwidth smaller than a critical band → loudness increases with bandwidth  
- Bandwidth larger than a critical band → loudness grows more slowly  

Critical bands reflect the cochlea’s frequency resolution and are tied to the mechanical filtering of hair cells.

<Callout type="success" title="Critical Band Reminder">
  The cochlea groups energy within those critical zones, so two narrow tones within one band may mask each other while the same tones separated by a critical band do not.
</Callout>

---

## 6. Introduction to Auditory Masking

Masking happens when one sound (the masker) renders another sound (the target) harder or impossible to hear. Limited frequency resolution and nonlinear cochlear processing cause masking, which explains why weak sounds vanish in noise and why audio coding can remove inaudible components.

<Callout type="info" title="Why Masking Matters">
  Masking proves that audibility depends on the neighborhood of the signal—the same tone may disappear next to a louder masker even if its SPL stays constant.
</Callout>

---

## 7. Simultaneous (Frequency) Masking

In **simultaneous masking**, masker and target overlap in time.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter04/subchapter02-7.1.png"
  caption="Simultaneous masking spreads more toward higher frequencies from the masker."
/>

Key characteristics:
- strongest when masker and target frequencies are close  
- masking spreads asymmetrically toward higher frequencies  
- broadband noise masks more efficiently than pure tones  

The frequency region affected by a masker is called the **masking pattern**.

<Callout type="warning" title="Masking Tip">
  Be mindful that a masker near the target’s frequency can easily hide it, especially when the masker is louder or broadband.
</Callout>

---

## 8. Temporal Masking

Masking also plays out across time.

Two forms:
- **Forward masking**: masker precedes target  
- **Backward masking**: masker follows target  

Forward masking is stronger and can last tens of milliseconds.

<ImageSection
  src="/images/chapter04/subchapter02-8.1.jpg"
  caption="Temporal masking shows how preceding or following sounds influence detectability."
/>

Temporal masking reflects the finite response time of the auditory system.

<Callout type="info" title="Temporal Reminder">
  The ear’s response stretches beyond the masker, so timing matters almost as much as spectral content when predicting audibility.
</Callout>

## 9. Practical Importance of Masking

Masking is fundamental in many applications:

- **Audio coding** (MP3, AAC): inaudible components are discarded  
- **Noise control**: tonal noise is more disturbing than broadband noise  
- **Auralization**: weak reflections may be perceptually irrelevant  
- **Hearing science**: masking thresholds map auditory filters  

<Callout type="info" title="Why It Matters">
  Engineers and researchers exploit masking to build efficient audio codecs, design comfortable spaces, and probe the limits of hearing.
</Callout>

---

## 10. Summary

<Callout type="info" title="Takeaway">
  Loudness and masking show that perception depends on more than just level—frequency, bandwidth, and surrounding sounds control what we hear.
</Callout>

- Loudness is perceptual and depends on frequency, level, and bandwidth  
- Equal-loudness contours describe frequency-dependent sensitivity  
- Loudness level (phons) and loudness (sones) use different scales to capture perception  
- Masking occurs when sounds obscure one another, both simultaneously and across time  
- Masking principles underpin audio coding, noise control, and auralization
